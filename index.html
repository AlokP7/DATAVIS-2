1 Australia's Domestic Aviation Network

Busiest Air Routes

The Melbourne–Sydney corridor is the world's third-busiest air route, carrying over 8 million passengers in 2024. The east-coast triangle — Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane — dominates domestic aviation.

The top three routes alone account for nearly half of all domestic passenger trips. This matters for fares: when competition collapses on even one of these corridors, it moves the national average meaningfully.

2 Inflation-adjusted fare history 2010–2026

Are Fares Getting Cheaper?

Adjusted for inflation, fares on the Melbourne–Sydney route more than doubled during COVID before easing back. But in real terms, many routes are more expensive in 2024 than in 2010.

The 2010s were a golden era for competition. Tigerair, Virgin, and Jetstar pushed real fares steadily downward. COVID ended that era abruptly — and the recovery has been uneven.

3 Distance · Fare · Value per kilometre

Which Routes Are Good Value?

There is a clear inverse relationship between distance and fare per kilometre. Long-haul routes are far cheaper per km than short hops.

Flying Perth–Sydney costs roughly $0.19 per km. The Canberra–Melbourne shuttle costs over $0.56 per km — nearly three times more.

4 Monthly patterns across cities and years

When Is the Cheapest Time to Fly?

Domestic fares follow clear seasonal rhythms. February is consistently the cheapest month on MEL–SYD. April and December school holidays push prices higher.

COVID shattered this pattern in 2020 — prices spiked even as passenger numbers collapsed. By 2023, the seasonal rhythm began to re-emerge, though at a higher baseline.

5 Volume · Load factor · Aircraft trips

How Busy Are Australia's Skies?

The MEL–SYD corridor grew almost continuously from deregulation in the 1980s — until COVID brought it to near-complete halt in April 2020. The recovery was remarkably fast. By 2023, monthly numbers were back above pre-COVID levels. But airlines recovered volume by running fewer flights, not more — deliberately constraining capacity to maintain high occupancy. Full planes at higher prices is more profitable than half-empty planes at lower ones.

01

Real fares are higher today than in 2010

Despite 14 years of economic growth and technological improvement in aviation, Australian domestic airfares adjusted for inflation are higher in 2024 than in 2010 on most major routes. The 2010s offered a brief golden era of falling real prices — driven by low-cost carrier expansion — but that era has ended. COVID was the shock, but the duopoly's post-COVID capacity discipline has been the sustaining mechanism.

02

Competition directly sets the price Australians pay

The data is unambiguous: every time a new operator entered a route, fares fell. Every time one exited, they rose. Tigerair's expansion in 2017 pushed MEL–SYD fares to their lowest real level in decades. Rex's entry in 2021 briefly did the same. Rex's collapse in July 2024 reversed those gains within months. Without ongoing competitive pressure, structural fare relief is not possible — regardless of fuel costs, aircraft efficiency, or macroeconomic conditions.

03

The capacity squeeze is a deliberate strategy

Post-COVID airlines did not simply respond to demand — they shaped it. By flying roughly 14% fewer aircraft trips than in 2019 while carrying the same number of passengers, Qantas and Virgin Australia have maintained load factors consistently above 85%. This is not a market recovering to equilibrium; it is a market operating under rational oligopoly discipline. Full planes at high prices are more profitable than full planes at low prices, and the surviving duopoly has no incentive to break that logic unilaterally.

04

Not all routes are equal — distance and competition both matter

The route value analysis reveals a clear hierarchy. Long-haul routes like Perth–Sydney offer the best value per kilometre (~$0.19/km), while short-haul routes in thin competitive markets — Canberra–Melbourne in particular — are systematically expensive (~$0.56/km). Regional travellers with fewer alternatives consistently pay more per kilometre than those on busy east-coast corridors. This inequality has widened since 2010, not narrowed.

05

For sustained relief, new competitive entry is necessary

The most important implication of this analysis is structural. Seasonal timing can save a traveller 15–20% on a given booking. But the only mechanism that has historically reduced fares meaningfully and durably is the entry of a new low-cost competitor — whether a domestic start-up, a revived regional carrier, or an international operator gaining cabotage rights. Without that, the data trajectory is clear: real fares will remain elevated, the capacity squeeze will persist, and Australians will continue to pay more, in real terms, than they did in 2010 for the same journey.

The bottom line: Australia's domestic aviation market is not broken — it is functioning exactly as an oligopoly should. The question for policymakers and consumers alike is whether that outcome is acceptable, and what structural changes could shift the equilibrium toward lower fares for the travelling public.

Australian domestic airfares, adjusted for inflation, are higher in 2024 than in 2010 on most major routes — despite 14 years of GDP growth and technological improvement. The primary driver is not cost increases but the progressive reduction of competitive pressure: the exit of Tigerair, the collapse of Rex and Bonza, and the post-COVID capacity discipline of the surviving duopoly.

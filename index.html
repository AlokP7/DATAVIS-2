The Changing Cost
of Flying in Australia

Fifteen years of BITRE data traces how Australian domestic airfares have shifted — from a long decade of falling real prices, through the COVID collapse, to an uncertain recovery. Are we flying cheaper than ever? The answer is more complicated than it looks.

Australia's Busiest Air Routes

The Melbourne–Sydney corridor is the world's third-busiest air route, carrying over 8 million passengers in 2024. The east-coast triangle — Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane — dominates domestic aviation. Long-haul routes to Perth and Cairns connect remote Australia to the major hubs.

02

Are Fares Getting Cheaper?

Adjusted for inflation, fares on the Melbourne–Sydney route more than doubled during COVID before easing back. But in real terms, many routes are actually more expensive in 2024 than in 2010 — driven by reduced competition after the collapse of Rex Airlines and Bonza in 2024.

03

Which Routes Are Good Value?

There is a clear inverse relationship between distance and fare per kilometre — long-haul routes are far cheaper per km than short hops. Flying Perth–Sydney costs roughly $0.19 per km, while the Canberra–Melbourne shuttle costs over $0.56 per km.

04

When Is the Cheapest Time to Fly?

Domestic fares follow clear seasonal rhythms. February is consistently the cheapest month on the Melbourne–Sydney route. April and December school holidays push prices higher. COVID shattered this pattern in 2020 — prices spiked even as passenger numbers collapsed.

Monthly real fare heatmap — Melbourne–Sydney, 2015–2026
Each cell = average real fare for that month and year. Darker blue = more expensive. Read across a row for the seasonal pattern within one year; read down a column to compare the same month across years.
Seasonal fare profile by departure city, 2024
Each panel = average monthly fare from that city across all routes in 2024. The seasonal shape is consistent across cities; the absolute level varies widely. Perth departures (~$400) cost roughly double Sydney departures (~$200).
05

How Busy Are Australia's Skies?

The Melbourne–Sydney corridor has grown almost continuously since deregulation in the 1980s — until COVID brought it to a near-complete halt in April 2020. The recovery was remarkably fast. By 2023, monthly passenger numbers were back above pre-COVID levels, setting new records by 2024.
